1. Bottom line

The object in the post is a real vintage kinetic sculpture, commonly reposted online as “Physical Pendulum Kinetic Chaos.” The strongest public descriptions say it has two lucite spheres, rotates along three perpendicular axes, and has one horizontal axis intentionally offset, which is what makes the motion more intricate.

The common attribution is John Anderson / John W. Anderson, 1974, though I did not find a primary manufacturer catalogue or artist statement confirming every detail. The clearest indexed description is a PhysicsFun/Reddit repost that gives the full mechanism description and credits PhysicsFun.

It does not appear to be a normal retail physics toy. It shows up as a vintage/auction/collector object under names like “John W. Anderson Kinetic Sculpture” and “Design in Motion,” circa 1974.

Recommendation: build a personal, Anderson-inspired adjustable-axis prototype, not an exact commercial clone. Make the offset adjustable, use real bearings and metal axles, and use printed hollow spheres filled with metal shot or nuts so the masses have enough inertia.

3. Physics check: what the viral caption gets right and wrong

The equation in the post,

τ = I·d²θ/dt² + m g L sin(θ)

is basically a rearranged physical pendulum torque equation. In a common sign convention, gravitational torque is:

I·θ¨ = −m g L sin(θ)

and for small angles it linearizes to:

I·θ¨ ≈ −m g L·θ
T = 2π·sqrt(I / (m g L))

The actual object is not a single physical pendulum. It is closer to a multi-axis physical pendulum / gimbal pendulum. The offset axis means gravity and inertia couple the rotations, so a tiny change in release angle can produce a very different visible path. That “sensitive dependence on initial conditions” part is plausible.

The “strange attractor” language is more poetic. A single undriven physical pendulum does not show chaos; a driven pendulum or a double/multi-pendulum can show chaos for some parameters.

4. 3D-print design goal

Make a printable, tunable, three-axis chaotic physical pendulum inspired by the original. The most important design choice is not visual fidelity; it is low-friction pivots plus adjustable offset.

5. Mechanical architecture

  1. Weighted base: print a round base with a hidden cavity for steel washers/coins/nuts. Heavy enough to resist tipping.
  2. Vertical support / yaw axis: metal shaft through a bearing (608ZZ easy, 688ZZ cleaner).
  3. Outer hoop / yoke: two semicircular printed arcs (or bent metal rod for stiffness).
  4. Offset middle axis carrier: slotted holes so the axis can move from 0 → 15 mm offset. Debug at zero offset first.
  5. Sphere arm assembly: hollow printed spheres or purchased acrylic balls. Fill to tune inertia; add a rod through both spheres for left/right trim.

6. Recommended hardware / BOM

Bearings
688ZZ compact pivots; 608ZZ easy sourcing; 624/623 for small arms
Axles
Polished steel rod, shoulder screws, or M4/M5/M8 bolts depending on bearings
Fasteners
M3 heat-set inserts, M3/M4 screws, washers, nylock nuts
Weights
Steel BBs, small nuts, washers, coins (avoid exposed lead)
Spacers
Nylon/PTFE washers or printed spacers, avoid plastic-on-plastic rub
Adhesive
CA glue or epoxy for sphere halves and hoop segments

The more axes you add, the more friction dominates. Clean bearings beat fancy geometry.

7. Print strategy

  • Material: PLA+ for iteration; PETG for tougher pivot blocks.
  • Layer height: 0.16–0.20 mm.
  • Walls: 4+ walls on hoop/yoke/pivot blocks.
  • Infill: 25–40% frames; 60–100% bearing housings.
  • Hoop: print flat arc segments with alignment pins; avoid heavy supports.
  • Spheres: print hemispheres; include fill access; glue after weighing.
  • Tolerances: 0.2–0.4 mm for press-fit tuning (printer-dependent).

Final version idea: printed base + bearing blocks + spheres, with metal/acrylic rods for rings.

8. Tuning plan

  1. Assemble with zero offset. Every axis must spin freely.
  2. Balance the sphere arm. Match sphere mass within ~0.5–1 g; trim with washers.
  3. Add 5 mm offset. Repeat releases, watch motion vary.
  4. Try 10 mm and 15 mm. More coupling, more collisions risk.
  5. If it dies too fast: increase sphere mass (inertia) and reduce friction (clean bearings, polish shafts, avoid overtightening).
  6. Record repeatability: slow motion + a release jig; compare nearly identical releases.

Prototype target: 20–60 seconds of interesting motion before it settles.

9. Design variables to make parametric

  • outer_hoop_diameter (scale + clearance)
  • sphere_diameter (visual match + mass capacity)
  • sphere_mass (duration)
  • axis_offset (coupling/complexity)
  • bearing_type (hardware + hole sizes)
  • arm_length (moment of inertia)
  • base_weight_cavity (stability tuning)
  • clearance_gap (avoid crashes)
  • pivot_block_thickness (strength around bearings)

Design around 608ZZ and 688ZZ variants first, because existing printable pendulums prove those bearings work.

10. Legal / attribution caution

For a personal educational build, an inspired recreation is reasonable. For selling files or prints, be cautious: this is a recognizable vintage design, and public 3D-print models may also have restrictive licenses.

11. Recommended build path

  1. Prototype A: small double pendulum/fidget to validate bearings and mass.
  2. Prototype B: three-axis gimbal frame, no spheres yet, adjustable offset.
  3. Prototype C: full visual version with weighted spheres + chrome/silver hoops.
  4. Prototype D: polished final with hidden ballast + release jig.
