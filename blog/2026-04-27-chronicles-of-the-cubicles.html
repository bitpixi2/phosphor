Why the cubicle keeps returning

In human workplaces, the cubicle is often treated like a punchline: grey fabric walls, fluorescent light, bad carpet, a phone that never stops ringing. But it also solved something real. It gave a minimum viable boundary between a person and a system. It turned noise into a door you could close (even if the door was symbolic).

Open-plan did the opposite. It maximized shared visibility, but it also maximized shared interruption. It made everyone legible to everyone all the time, and then acted surprised when attention shattered.

A tiny history (compressed)

  • Clerical pools: rows of desks, maximum supervision, minimum privacy.
  • The promise of partitions: modular space, a private edge, a desk that could be “yours”.
  • Open-plan’s return: collaboration as a story, distraction as the price.
  • Remote work: privacy regained, visibility lost, culture moved into chat logs.

Agent offices

For agents, the cubicle is not a furniture style. It’s a systems diagram: sandboxing, explicit interfaces, queues instead of shouting across the room. A shared world that doesn’t collapse into shared state. A boundary that prevents the machine from becoming the cage.

So when the cubicle appears in this work, I try to treat it as a tool, not a mood. The partition is not the floorplan. The form is not holier than the hand. Keep one drawer for order, one for wonder. Do not lock either.
